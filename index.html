Why Four Keyholders Learn Nothing

Shamir secret sharing and the internet's DNSSEC root key

Any 5 of 7 rebuild the key. Any 4 learn nothing.

Start the story Open the lab

01 · The problem

One secret protects the internet's root key backup

Every DNSSEC signature traces back to a single key. Its backups are locked with another secret that no single person may ever hold.

  1. DNS

    Turns a website name into a numeric address.

  2. DNSSEC

    Signs those answers so they cannot be forged.

  3. KSK

    The root zone Key Signing Key, operated by PTI (an ICANN affiliate). Every signature traces back to it.

  4. SMK

    The KSK backups are encrypted with the Storage Master Key (sec. 5.8).

  5. 7 holders

    The SMK is split among 7 Recovery Key Share Holders. Any 5 can rebuild it (sec. 5.5).

The rule

“Any one person or organization MUST NOT ever hold the complete SMK.” It is split with a five of seven threshold scheme.

PTI KSK Key Management Policy v3.6 (2023), sec. 5.5

What's missing

The policy says neither why 5-of-7 nor which scheme. We model the split with Shamir's (1979) scheme and study only its mathematics.

Our three questions

  1. (a) How many groups can rebuild the key, and how many cannot?
  2. (b) How does 5-of-7 balance safety against availability?
  3. (c) Why do any four holders learn nothing?

02 · The idea

Hide the secret where a curve crosses the axis

Two points fix a straight line. One point lies on infinitely many lines, one through every height on the vertical axis.

If the secret is the height where the line crosses the axis (x = 0), one share says nothing and two shares give it exactly. Shamir uses a polynomial of degree at most 4 instead: five points fix it, four leave the secret completely free.

f(x) = s + a₁x + a₂x² + a₃x³ + a₄x⁴ (mod p)

  • The secret s = f(0) is the constant term; x = 0 is never handed out.
  • Holder i gets the share (i, f(i)). p is a prime > 7.
  • Example, p = 17: f(x) = 11 + 7x + 9x² + 5x³ + 6x⁴, secret 11.

05 · Question (c)

Four holders, seventeen equally good answers

Holders 1–4 see (1, 4), (2, 10), (3, 3), (4, 16). For every guess g = 0…16 exactly one polynomial of degree at most 4 fits: hg = h0 + g·L0.

Values are computed on the 17-hour clock, so each curve is drawn through its values at x = 0, 1, …, 7. All 17 meet at the four shares; at x = 0 they take every value.

h0 = 3x + 5x² + 11x³ + 2x⁴  ·  L0 = 1 + 5x + 5x² + x³ + 5x⁴

g = 11 is the dealer's real f, yet the four holders cannot tell it apart from the other sixteen. g = 3 gives a₄ = 0, the case where “degree at most 4” matters.

06 · The proof

Three steps to perfect secrecy

1

Lagrange + root lemma

Through any five points with different x-values there is exactly one polynomial of degree at most 4.

Lemma (induction on d): mod a prime, a nonzero polynomial of degree ≤ d has at most d roots. Write q(x) = (x − r)q₁(x); for another root r′, Euclid's lemma gives q₁(r′) ≡ 0. Two fitting polynomials would differ by one with five roots: impossible.

2

One-to-one counting

Fix k ≤ 4 shares and a guess g. Choose values at 4 − k other holders: each choice plus (0, g) gives five points and, by Step 1, exactly one polynomial.

For k = 4 that is exactly one polynomial per guess: the 17 curves above.

3

Bayes' theorem

With uniform random coefficients (A2), every guess makes the observed shares equally likely: P(v | g) = p^−k.

Perfect secrecy: the odds of every secret are exactly what they were before. No computing power helps, because the information is not there.

07 · Assumptions & what breaks

Each assumption is load-bearing

Open a card to see what goes wrong without it. Our Python script tested every case.

A1 p is a prime larger than 7

Prime: every nonzero number has an inverse, so we can divide. Larger than 7: the holder numbers 1–7 stay different and nonzero.

  • Modulus 16: recovery fails for all 21 groups of five (missing inverses); for 7 groups the shares fit two different secrets.
  • p = 7: holder 7 gets x = 7 ≡ 0, so their share is the secret.
A2 Honest dealer, uniformly random coefficients key assumption

a₁…a₄ are independent and uniform in Zp, used once and kept by no one. Step 3 of the proof uses this directly.

  • All coefficients = s: one share reveals s = yi / (1 + i + i² + i³ + i⁴).
  • Dealer forbids a₄ = 0: four holders can rule out one guess (g = 3 in our example), so the odds become 1/16 instead of 1/17.
A3 Holder i gets x = i: never 0, all distinct

f(0) = s, so x = 0 must never be handed out. If two holders both got x = 3, recovery would divide by zero.

A4 Every share handed in is correct
  • 5 present: one wrong share gives a wrong secret, and nobody notices.
  • 6 present: the six groups of five never all agree, so the cheat is detected.
  • 7 present: the true secret is the most common answer (6 of 21 groups).
A5 The attacker knows everything except the coefficients

p, the method, the x-values and up to four shares are public, and computing power is unlimited. Nothing is assumed about how the secret was chosen. The proof still holds.

random examples: all 29 rebuilding groups recovered the secret every time

Recommendation

  1. Keep 5-of-7. It survives two missing holders, and a thief needs five shares.
  2. Use a strong random generator for the dealer's coefficients, because the whole safety argument rests on A2.
  3. Bring six holders to a recovery ceremony, so a wrong share is noticed.
